LRDI War Room

LRDI is the most rank-deciding section of CAT — it rewards set selection and disciplined solving far more than raw brilliance. Here is the complete playbook.

The golden rule: set selection wins the section
  • CAT LRDI gives ~4-5 sets; you typically need only 2.5–3 fully-correct sets for a 99 percentile. The section is a selection test disguised as a solving test.
  • Spend the first 3-4 minutes scanning every set. Rate each: (a) do I recognise the structure? (b) is the data self-contained? (c) are the questions mostly deterministic (single answer forced by conditions) or "cannot be determined" traps?
  • Order of attack: familiar structure + short data → familiar structure + long data → unfamiliar. Never start with the first set just because it's first.
  • Sunk-cost discipline: if a set gives you nothing concrete in 4-5 minutes, abandon it without guilt. A half-solved set scores zero; the time is worth ~12 marks elsewhere.
  • Within a chosen set, do all questions — the expensive part (decoding the set) is already paid for. Leaving a set after 2 of 4 questions wastes your setup cost.
Arrangements (linear, circular, grids)
  • Draw first, read second. Sketch the row/circle/grid skeleton before processing clues.
  • Process clues in two passes: pass 1 places the definite facts (fixed positions, "extreme left", "opposite"); pass 2 applies relative clues, branching into at most 2-3 cases.
  • Circular tables: fix one person's seat to kill rotational duplicates. Watch facing direction — for people facing the centre, "left" is clockwise; facing outward, it reverses.
  • Keep a negative-information margin: "B not at ends", "C ≠ red" — these often finish the puzzle when positive clues run out.
  • If two cases survive, carry both in parallel columns; often a single question clue collapses one.
Data Interpretation (tables, graphs, caselets)
  • Read the fine print first: units, footnotes, whether figures are cumulative, percentages of what base. Most DI errors are base errors, not calculation errors.
  • Approximate aggressively: options are usually far apart. 90/260 → "a bit less than 90/258 ≈ 35%". Only compute exactly when options are tight or it's TITA.
  • Memorise: fraction↔percentage table (1/6…1/15), squares to 32, cubes to 15, and reciprocals — this is your on-paper calculator.
  • Percentage change comparisons: compare change⁄base as fractions rather than computing each to decimals.
  • For "how many years/products satisfy X" questions, scan for a counting shortcut before computing every cell.
Games, tournaments & sports sets
  • Anchor with conservation totals: total points = 2 × matches (win-loss) or matches × points-per-match; total goals scored = total conceded. Verify your grid against these before answering.
  • Round robin of n teams: n(n−1)/2 matches. Knockout of n players: n − 1 matches total.
  • Build the results matrix (teams × teams) and fill both symmetric cells with every clue.
  • Extreme cases first: the team that won everything / lost everything constrains everyone else.
Venn diagrams & set-based caselets
  • Always work with the innermost region outward: fill "all three" first, then "exactly two", then "exactly one".
  • Know both identities: |A∪B∪C| = ΣA − Σpairs + triple, and Total = exactly-one + exactly-two + exactly-three + none.
  • Maximisation/minimisation questions: to maximise "none", maximise overlap; to minimise an intersection, spread the sets apart. Check feasibility against every constraint.
  • 4-set problems: use a table of 16 regions rather than trying to draw 4 circles.
Selection & grouping conditions
  • Translate every condition into symbols first: "A and B can't be together" → ¬(A∧B); "if C then D" → C→D (note: D can come without C!).
  • The most-constrained element is your entry point — start casework on it.
  • "If-then" traps: C→D forbids only (C without D). Both present, both absent, and D-alone are all legal.
  • Count systematically by cases (by group size or by inclusion/exclusion of the pivotal member) and check totals with C(n,r) sanity bounds.
Preparation plan for LRDI
  • 2 sets a day, every day, alternating LR and DI — consistency beats weekend binges.
  • After every set: log the set type, time taken, and the breakthrough observation you missed. Your error log becomes your personal strategy book.
  • Redo failed sets after a week from scratch. If you fail again, the set type goes on your "recognise and skip in exam" list — knowing what NOT to attempt is a legitimate skill.
  • Sectional mocks (start here) under strict time > untimed practice, always, from month one.
  • Puzzle cross-training that genuinely helps: Sudoku, Kakuro, and past CAT/XAT sets — not random riddle apps.
Drill it now: The LRDI sectional mocks contain 10 sets across all these types. Attempt one mock, then read this page again — the strategies will land differently once you've bled a little.
